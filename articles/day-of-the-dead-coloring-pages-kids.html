Día de los Muertos Coloring Pages for Kids: A Gentle Way to Talk About Remembering

The candy buckets are barely back in the cupboard when the calendar turns to November 1. Halloween is over, the costumes are in a heap by the door, and your kids are already asking what comes next. If you live anywhere with a Mexican community — or if your kids have classmates who celebrate it — the answer might be Día de los Muertos, the Day of the Dead. And the first question out of your child's mouth will probably be: "Is it Halloween again?"

It is not, and the difference is worth getting right. Halloween is about dressing up and spooky fun. Día de los Muertos, celebrated on November 1 and 2, is about remembering. Families in Mexico, and Mexican families everywhere, spend these two days thinking about the people they loved who have died — grandparents, great-uncles, a beloved aunt — and welcoming their memory back into the house for a visit. There are marigolds everywhere, candles burning, photographs set out carefully, and the favourite foods of the people being remembered. It is warm, and a little bit sad, and mostly joyful. Kids tend to understand it faster than grown-ups do, because nobody has told them yet that remembering someone is supposed to be a heavy, hushed thing.

What your kids will actually see (and ask about)

If your children see Día de los Muertos images for the first time — at school, in a shop window, on a neighbour's porch — they will notice four things, and they will have questions about all of them. Here is the plain version of each one, so you are not caught off guard in the cereal aisle.

Marigolds. Those big orange flowers are everywhere during Día de los Muertos. The tradition says their bright colour and strong scent help guide loved ones home. Petals are scattered in paths from the door to the altar. When your child asks why there are flowers all over the pavement, that is the answer: they are making a path, so nobody gets lost on the way back.

Sugar skulls. The decorated skulls look spooky at first glance, and they are the thing most kids fixate on. But a sugar skull is not meant to be scary. Skulls made of sugar, decorated with bright icing flowers, are set out on altars with the names of the people being remembered written across the forehead. They are cheerful on purpose. Death is part of the picture, but it is drawn in bright colours, not shadows. If your child is nervous about the skull, say exactly that: it is a decoration, like a birthday cake with a name on it, made for someone we miss.

Candles and photos. An ofrenda is a small altar — a table, sometimes just a shelf — with photographs of the people being remembered, candles, marigolds, and food. The candlelight is practical in the old sense and symbolic in the new one: light for the journey home. The photos are the point. The whole celebration is built around looking at faces and telling stories about them.

Music and food. There is singing, there is guitar music, and there is a lot of cooking. Families make the dishes their loved ones liked best — pan de muerto, a sweet bread made for the occasion, sugar skulls, tamales, hot chocolate. The idea is simple and lovely: you remember someone by making what they made, and eating what they loved.

How to talk about it without making it heavy

You do not need a script for this conversation, and you definitely do not need to turn it into a lesson. The kids who handle this topic best are the ones whose parents answered the question that was actually asked, in about three sentences, and then stopped. "We are remembering people we loved who died. We put out their photos and their favourite food, and we tell stories about them. It makes us happy and a little bit sad, and both of those are okay." That is the whole talk, for most children, most of the time.

Let them lead. Some kids will ask one question and go back to their toast. Some will ask twenty, including the one every parent dreads: "Will you die?" Answer that one honestly and simply, in the way your family talks about such things: everyone dies someday, and that is why we remember people and tell their stories. You do not need to solve the whole mystery of life before breakfast. You just need to not dodge the question, because children can tell when an adult is dodging, and dodging is what makes a topic scary.

If your own family has lost someone recently, go gently and follow your child's lead. Some children want to talk and talk. Some want to draw a picture, put it down, and go and play. Both are fine. There is no right amount of remembering, and there is no deadline on it. And if your family has not lost anyone close, you can still talk about it — about a great-grandparent your child never met, or a neighbour everyone liked, or simply about how different families in different places remember the people they love. That last one is the real gift of this holiday for kids who do not celebrate it at home: it shows them, early, that there is more than one way to love someone after they are gone.

Why coloring works better than a lesson

Here is the practical part. Sitting a five-year-old down for a talk about death and memory is a hard sell. Handing that same five-year-old a crayon and a sugar skull to decorate is not. Colouring keeps small hands busy and gives the questions somewhere to land sideways, which is how children prefer to ask hard things — while they are doing something else, without eye contact, in their own time.

That is what our free Day of the Dead coloring pages are for. Six designs on one page, thick outlines, big shapes, nothing fussy. Print it, put the crayon box in the middle of the table, and let the conversation happen or not happen. Either way, your child has spent twenty minutes with marigolds and candles and a guitar, and the holiday will not feel strange or frightening when they meet it again.

The six designs on the printable

A simple thing to try at home

If you want to do one small thing beyond the colouring, try a memory table. It takes fifteen minutes and no shopping. Find a photograph of someone your family remembers — a grandparent, an old family friend, anyone with a story attached. Put the photo on a shelf or a side table. Let your child draw a flower for it, or pick a flower from the garden if you have one. Put out a small plate with a snack that person liked, if you know one. Then tell one story about them. Just one, and keep it short: the funny thing they always said, the food they always burned, the song they always sang. Then let your child tell a story too, even if it is one they only half remember. That is the whole activity. It is not a ceremony, and it does not need to be perfect. It is a child learning that the people we love do not disappear when we stop seeing them — they live in the stories we keep telling.

A note for teachers

If you are bringing this into a classroom, the framing matters more than the activity. Start with what the holiday is, in the same plain terms: families remembering people they loved, with flowers and candles and photos. Keep it to five minutes, keep it factual, and keep the focus on remembering, not on anything spooky. The colouring pages work well as a calm follow-up to the discussion, and they pair with a simple writing prompt for older children: draw or write about someone you remember, or about a family tradition of your own. One caution: sugar skull face painting is part of the real celebration, but in a classroom it lands better as paper art than as dress-up. Colour the skull on the page, and leave the make-up out of it.

The bottom line

Día de los Muertos is not a second Halloween, and it is not something to tiptoe around. It is a celebration built on a simple idea that children grasp immediately: we remember the people we love, out loud, with flowers and food and music, and remembering them is a happy thing to do. Print the colouring pages, hand over the crayons, answer the questions that come, and let the rest happen on its own. If one of those questions is hard, that is fine too. The hard questions are the ones worth answering, and a sugar skull in bright orange is a much friendlier place to start than a lecture.
